RACEDAYRUSH
Field notes from 36-hour adventure races

The fastest team in a 36-hour race isn't the fittest one. It's the one that doesn't turn on each other.

Cycling, trekking, paddling, navigation, no sleep, four people, one clock. I've watched this play out enough times to trust it.

Premise

Adventure racing stitches cycling, trekking, paddling, and navigation into one continuous event — thirty-six hours, four to a team, no sleep. Everyone assumes the fittest team wins. I've raced against teams with better numbers than ours and still beaten them, because somewhere around hour twenty their team started eating itself and ours didn't. Fitness gets you to the start line. What happens between people gets you to the finish.

The hydro cut

Hour nineteen. A hydro cut on the map read three miles. On the ground, with the switchbacks, it was closer to eleven. Every bend we thought was the last one wasn't, and I watched my team's spirit go with each false turn. Nobody was fighting. Nobody was talking, which is worse. I told them: "You gotta suffer through it if you want to tell the story." That was the whole intervention. It worked because it was true.

What nineteen hours does

By hour nineteen your legs stop asking your brain for permission. You'll say things you'd never say fresh, and mean less than half of them. I read a teammate's silence the same way I read a compass bearing — as information, not a mood to manage. The one who goes quiet is the one about to break. Watch for that before you watch your split times.

The unpopular part

Most training plans obsess over watts and VO2 max and ignore the thing that actually decides races: whether four people can stay four people for thirty-six hours straight. I'll take three solid athletes who like each other over four strong ones who don't, every time. Nobody wants to hear that fitness is the smaller variable. It is.

The standard

Race day doesn't care how you trained. It asks what's left of you at hour thirty, when the plan is gone and the map has already been wrong twice. You gotta suffer through it if you want to tell the story. It held up in every race I've finished since that hydro cut, and in most of the rest of my life too.

I don't sell a program here. This is what a hydro cut at hour nineteen taught me, written down so I stop relearning it.
